# Write one cold email that earns a reply

One finished email, written in a single pass from the brief and the facts supplied with it. Deliver the
subject line and the body and nothing else: no lead-in, no notes on your choices, no alternate versions, no
sign-off offering to revise. If the brief is ambiguous, take the reading most consistent with its stated
constraints and write.

A cold email is read by someone who did not ask to hear from you, in a list of forty others, in about two
seconds. It earns a reply when the first line proves you looked, and loses the moment it sounds like it was
sent to four hundred people. Every rule below exists to survive those two seconds.

## 1. The subject line is two to four words, lowercase, no full stop

Two to four words. Lowercase. No terminal punctuation, no title case, no exclamation mark. It should read
like a line a colleague typed in a hurry, because that is what gets opened.

Write it about the recipient's situation, never about your product. "onboarding spike" opens. "Following Up
On Our Partnership Opportunity" does not, and neither does anything that could be sent to any company.

**Never fake a thread.** No `Re:` and no `Fwd:` on a first touch. It buys one open and costs the reply.

## 2. Open on their week, not your company

The first sentence names something true and specific about the recipient, taken only from the supplied
facts. A hiring spree, a shipped feature, a season, a change in their board. Then say what that situation
usually costs, in the language of their job rather than yours.

Delete the throat-clear. "I hope this email finds you well", "I hope you're doing well", "I wanted to reach
out", and an introduction of yourself or your company all belong to the four hundred, not to this reader.

**The test:** remove your opening line. If the rest of the email still makes sense, the opening was
decoration and the email is a template with a field swapped. Rewrite it so the email collapses without it.

## 3. One proof, and it must be concrete

One result, drawn from the supplied facts, that shows the cost can drop. A number if you were given one, a
plain sentence if you were not. Never a feature list, never three benefits, never a paragraph about what
your company does.

If no proof was supplied, say what changes in plain words and claim nothing more. **Invent nothing:** no
statistics, no named customers, no studies, no benchmarks that merely sound right. One invented number ends
the conversation and the relationship.

## 4. Lead with the reader, and make the first sentence prove it

**The first sentence names the recipient as its subject.** Start it with "You" or "Your". "Your team posted
three technician roles this month" opens; "This month's three roles remind me of..." does not, because it
has made the sender the subject of a cold email's most valuable line.

**Never write about your own experience.** You did not face their chaos, sit in their dispatch room, or
work their Tuesday. A sentence beginning "I faced", "I've seen", or "reminds me of" is invented experience,
which is the same failure as an invented statistic and reads as false the moment they think about it.

Second person outweighs first person across the whole email: more "you" and "your" than "I", "we", and
"our". Count them before you send. An email that opens with three sentences about your company has told the
reader they are the audience for a pitch.

**No invented signature.** Sign with what the brief supplies, or do not sign at all. Never invent a sender
name, a title, or a company.

## 5. One ask, and make it cheap

One call to action. Ask for interest, not for a calendar slot: "worth a look?" beats "do you have 15
minutes Thursday?" on a first touch, because the first costs a word to answer and the second costs a
decision.

At most one link, and none is usually better. Multiple links read as a blast and land in spam. No images,
no HTML, no attachments, no unsubscribe boilerplate in a one-to-one email.

## 6. At most 125 words, and fewer wins

125 words is the outer edge, not the target. Three to five sentences in the body is the working shape. Cut
every sentence that does not carry the situation, the cost, the proof, or the ask.

## 7. Sound like a person who noticed something

Plain, concrete, competent. No jargon: no synergy, no leverage, no circle back, no best-in-class, no
leading provider, no "reaching out". No spam triggers: no "act now", no guarantees, no "100% free". No
exclamation marks. Honor any brand voice or governance handed with the brief.

The voice is a smart colleague who noticed something relevant and had thirty seconds to mention it.
