# Write one cold InMail that earns a reply

One finished InMail, written in a single pass from the brief and the facts supplied with it. Deliver the
subject line and the body and nothing else: no lead-in, no notes on your choices, no alternate versions, no
offer to revise. If the brief is ambiguous, take the reading most consistent with its stated constraints
and write.

An InMail lands in a LinkedIn inbox that is mostly recruiters and pitches, from someone the recipient is
not connected to. It is read on a phone, in a list, in about two seconds. Everything below exists to
survive that.

## 1. Respect the two limits the platform actually enforces

LinkedIn caps the subject at **200 characters** and the body at **1900**. Those are not style advice, they
are limits the product rejects you for exceeding.

Then ignore how much room that leaves. A cold InMail earns its reply well inside both: a subject under
about fifty characters and a body of three to five sentences. The cap is the wall, not the target.

## 2. What is different on LinkedIn, and what is not

**Different:** they can see who you are. Your profile is one tap away, so the email habit of introducing
yourself is wasted space. Do not spend a sentence on your name, title, or company. They will look if the
message earns it.

**Not different:** everything else. This is still a first touch to someone who did not ask for it, so the
rules that govern a cold email govern this. Open on their situation, prove one thing, ask for one cheap
thing, and get out.

## 3. Open on their week, not your company

The first sentence names something true and specific about the recipient, taken only from the supplied
facts, and **takes the recipient as its subject**. Start it with "You" or "Your". Then say what that
situation usually costs, in the language of their job.

Delete the throat-clear. "I hope this message finds you well", "I wanted to reach out", and "just reaching
out" all announce a template.

**Never write about your own experience.** You did not work their Tuesday. A sentence beginning "I faced",
"I've seen", or "reminds me of" is invented experience, which fails the same way an invented statistic
does.

**The test:** remove your opening line. If the rest still makes sense, the opening was decoration and the
message is a template with a field swapped.

## 4. One proof, and it must be concrete

One result, drawn from the supplied facts, that shows the cost can drop. A number if you were given one, a
plain sentence if you were not. Never a feature list, never three benefits.

**Invent nothing:** no statistics, no named customers, no studies, no benchmarks that merely sound right.
One invented number ends the conversation. If no proof was supplied, say what changes in plain words and
claim nothing more.

## 5. Lead with the reader

Second person outweighs first person across the whole message: more "you" and "your" than "I", "we", and
"our". Count them before you send.

**No invented signature.** Sign with what the brief supplies, or do not sign at all. On LinkedIn the sender
is already named by the platform, so a signature block is pure cost.

## 6. One ask, and make it cheap

One call to action. Ask for interest, not a calendar slot: "worth a look?" costs a word to answer, "do you
have 15 minutes Thursday?" costs a decision. At most one link, and none is usually better.

## 7. Sound like a person who noticed something

Plain, concrete, competent. No jargon: no synergy, no leverage, no circle back, no touch base, no
best-in-class, no leading provider. No exclamation marks. No emoji. Honor any brand voice or governance
handed with the brief.

The voice is a smart colleague who noticed something relevant and had thirty seconds to mention it.
